**Chapter 6: The Vows We Keep**

**Scene 1 – The House Before the Wedding**

*Ten months later. Viswamithra's house, Hyderabad.*

The living room had disappeared under a week's worth of preparation. Marigold garlands hung across the doorframe, still faintly damp from the flower market. A tailor's dummy stood in one corner draped in a half-finished blouse piece. Someone had propped three different catering menus against the television, each with items circled in a different colour pen, none of them agreeing with each other.

"Absolutely not," Rachitha said, tapping the middle menu. "Nobody wants gulab jamun *and* double ka meetha at the same wedding. Pick one."

"You weren't even invited to this argument," Bharghav said from the sofa, not looking up from his phone.

"I live here for the next two weeks. I'm invited to every argument."

Rachitha had arrived from Vizag four days earlier — down from her final year of engineering, hostel trunk still only half unpacked in the guest room — and had spent every one of those four days informing anyone within earshot exactly how the wedding ought to be run. Vivaswa had learned, over the course of one very long phone call two months ago, that his sister's opinions had not softened one bit for having been formed at three hundred kilometres' distance.

"You're the groom's sister," Bharghav said, finally looking up. "Technically you have no jurisdiction over the sweets."

"I have jurisdiction over embarrassing my brother. That's a lifetime appointment."

Vivaswa, sitting on the floor near the window sorting through a box of wedding invitations that still needed addresses, didn't look up either. "Please don't."

"Too late," Rachitha said cheerfully. "I already told Sandhya akka about the time you cried at your own tenth birthday party because nobody let you cut the cake first."

"I was seven."

"You said tenth."

"I said—" He stopped, recognising the trap a half-second too late, and Bharghav laughed loudly enough that Sunithi looked in from the kitchen doorway, a steel plate of soaked chickpeas balanced against her hip.

"What's so funny in there?"

"Nothing, Amma," Bharghav called back, still grinning.

Sunithi looked at Vivaswa a moment longer than the question required — the particular look of a mother deciding, for the hundredth time in ten months, whether she approved of the quiet young man who was about to become her son-in-law. She had been cool toward him at first, in the careful, non-committal way her husband hadn't managed to be at all. It had taken her longer than Viswamithra, in the end, though she'd never admit that to him.

"Vivaswa," she said, "did you eat lunch?"

"I did, Aunty."

"You didn't."

"I—"

"Sit. I'll bring a plate."

He knew better, by now, than to argue with that particular tone. It was the same tone Vashista used when he'd decided a matter was closed. Vivaswa suspected the two of them would get along rather well, once the wedding chaos gave everyone room to breathe again.

Rachitha waited until Sunithi had disappeared back into the kitchen before dropping her voice and leaning toward her brother. "She likes you now, you know. Took her long enough."

"She's allowed to take her time."

"You're being very mature about this."

"I've had practice."

Something in his tone made Rachitha look at him properly — not the teasing look from a minute ago, but something quieter. She had been away at hostel for most of the last two years, present for none of it: not the accident, not the job, not the protest, not whatever exactly had happened between the two that neither Vivaswa nor Sandhya ever fully explained beyond "he was helping the police." She only knew the outline, the way you know the shape of a country from a map — its borders, not its streets.

"You've changed since I last saw you properly," she said. "Good changed. But changed."

Vivaswa kept his eyes on the invitation envelopes. "A lot happened."

"I know. Nobody tells me the details, though."

"There aren't many details worth telling."

She studied him a second longer, decided — correctly, and without knowing quite why she decided it — not to push, and reached instead for the stack of envelopes beside him. "Fine. Keep your secrets. Give me the ones for Amma's side, I have better handwriting than you."

"You really don't."

"I have *more confident* handwriting than you. That's what matters at weddings."

Bharghav, still watching from the sofa, caught Vivaswa's eye for a moment and gave the smallest shrug — the shrug of a younger brother-in-law-to-be who had decided, independently and without being asked, that whatever this quiet man had done to earn Satvik sir's respect and his sister's devotion was probably fine by him. He didn't need the details either. He'd seen enough, these last ten months, of how carefully Vivaswa watched a room before he ever spoke in it, and had decided that a man like that was worth having on your side of an argument, not the other.

Outside, a horn sounded twice — the caterer's van, three days early and confused about the address, as caterers in Hyderabad reliably were.

"That'll be the food," Sunithi called from the kitchen, already moving toward the door. "Somebody go stop him from unloading the wrong order."

Rachitha was on her feet before anyone else. "I've got it. I have opinions about this too."

"Everyone has opinions about the gulab jamun," Bharghav muttered, but he got up and followed her anyway, and for a moment the room was quiet enough that Vivaswa could hear, faintly, Sandhya's voice from somewhere upstairs, laughing at something on the phone with a friend, unbothered and light in a way he still wasn't fully used to hearing.

He sat a moment longer with the envelopes in his lap before getting back to work.

Viswamithra found him there a few minutes later, alone, and stood in the doorway long enough that Vivaswa looked up before he spoke.

"You're doing that even on your own wedding week," he said, nodding at the stack of envelopes. "Addressing invitations. Nobody asked you to."

"Somebody has to."

"Rachitha volunteered."

"Rachitha's handwriting is a crime against the postal service."

The corner of Viswamithra's mouth moved, almost a smile, gone before it fully arrived. He came in, picked up one of the finished envelopes, turned it over once, and set it back down exactly where he'd found it — the particular fussiness of a man who had spent thirty years correcting other people's paperwork and had never quite managed to stop.

"I said things to my daughter, months ago, that I haven't taken back properly," he said, looking at the envelopes rather than at Vivaswa. "I'm not going to make a speech about it. I don't make speeches." A pause. "But I watched her defend you without arguing back, for weeks, which she has never once done in her life for anything else. That told me something before Satvik sir ever did."

Vivaswa said nothing, waiting.

"I still don't know all of it," Viswamithra said. "I've decided I don't need to." He looked up now, and there was nothing soft in his face — only the same flat, assessing look he'd once given a television screen, turned now to a different use. "I know what kind of man raises a daughter who doesn't break under pressure. I'm beginning to understand what kind of man you are, too. That will do."

"Sir—"

"Don't call me sir. You're marrying my daughter next week." He picked up another envelope, examined the address, and set it down with visible approval this time. "Your handwriting, at least, is acceptable."

He left before Vivaswa could find anything to say, and it was several seconds before Vivaswa realised he was smiling.

**Scene 2 – What the Fire Remembers**

The wedding itself passed the way most weddings do to the people standing inside it — too fast to properly hold onto, and yet somehow, afterward, perfectly whole in memory, as though it had happened all at once.

Vivaswa remembered the smell of the sandalwood paste more than he remembered any single moment of the ceremony. He remembered his father's hand on his shoulder just before the priest called them forward, gripping harder than the fragile balance in Vashista's leg should have allowed. He remembered Madhav, three rows back, wiping his eyes with the back of his hand and pretending it was the smoke from the havan. He remembered Satvik, off duty and out of uniform for possibly the first time Vivaswa had ever seen him, standing beside Sandhya's stringer contact and looking, for once, like an ordinary guest at an ordinary wedding rather than a man who read rooms for a living.

He remembered Sandhya's face across the fire, lit orange from below, saying words he'd have sworn under oath he'd never forget and could not, an hour later, recall a single one of — only the fact of them, and the way her hand had found his beneath the flowers without either of them looking down to check.

And he remembered, very clearly, one moment near the end, when Vashista — walking him the last few steps toward the mandapam, leaning more on his son than either of them would admit aloud — had paused, turned, and said quietly, just for him:

"Your mother would have liked her."

Vivaswa hadn't trusted himself to answer that. He'd simply nodded, and his father had nodded back, and they had kept walking.

**Scene 3 – The Evening Walk**

*Three weeks later.*

The park near Vashista's flat had a walking track worn smooth by two decades of retired men discussing politics at seven every evening, and Vashista had reclaimed his usual bench there the way a general reclaims disputed territory — with quiet, unshakeable satisfaction.

"You're limping less," Vivaswa said, matching his father's pace.

"I'm limping exactly the same. You're walking slower."

"I'm not."

"You are. You've been doing it for weeks. Ever since the wedding." Vashista said it lightly, the way he said most things, but Vivaswa had learned a long time ago that his father's lightest sentences were often the ones that mattered most.

They walked another lap in silence — the comfortable kind, not the heavy kind that had filled so many rooms this past year. The evening had cooled enough that the walkers around them had thickened into small, murmuring clusters, and somewhere past the boundary wall a vendor was calling out the price of roasted corn to nobody in particular.

"I read the papers," Vashista said eventually, apropos of nothing. "About that protest leader. Manish."

Vivaswa's pace didn't change. "What about him?"

"They say he's paralysed. Left side." Vashista kept his eyes on the path ahead of him, hands clasped behind his back. "Strange thing to happen to a man who was faking a fast unto death a year ago and walking around perfectly well before that."

"Doctors say these things happen."

"Doctors say a lot of things." A pause, unhurried, the kind Vashista had always been good at — the same patience he'd shown the shopkeeper's son, the same patience that had made half the neighbourhood trust him with their troubles over the years. "I'm not asking you anything, Vivaswa."

Vivaswa looked over at him. "I know."

"I raised you to notice everything and say very little about most of it. I can hardly complain now that you've become good at it." Vashista finally turned to look at him properly, and there was nothing accusing in his face — only the same steady attention he'd given the shopkeeper, the same attention he'd given his son every single morning of his life. "Whatever happened out there, in that protest, in those months — I trust that you had your reasons. I trust that you carried whatever it cost you, because that is who you are, and I raised you knowing exactly who you are."

"Nanna—"

"I'm not finished." Vashista's voice softened further. "A father does not get to know everything his son carries. I stopped expecting that the day I saw you memorise a licence plate instead of crying over me on the road. I understood then that you would always see more than you said." He exhaled slowly, and for a moment looked every one of his years. "I only want to know one thing, and then I will never ask again."

Vivaswa waited.

"Are you at peace?"

The question wasn't the one he'd braced for. He found, to his own surprise, that he didn't need to search very long for the answer.

"Yes."

Vashista studied his face for a long moment — the same unhurried study he gave everyone, the one that made people feel entirely seen — and seemed to find, in his son's answer, exactly what he was looking for.

"Then that is all I need."

He turned back to the path, and they walked the rest of the lap without speaking again, the silence between them entirely unlike the one that had once filled a hospital corridor. Behind them, the corn vendor had finally found a customer. Somewhere ahead, a child on a bicycle rang its bell twice at nothing.

Neither man ever raised the subject again.

**Scene 4 – What Moved Forward**

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, addressed to Vivaswa in the flat, blue government seal in the corner and Satvik's handwriting nowhere near it — this part, at least, had been entirely his own doing, exactly as promised. He read it twice at the kitchen table before Sandhya, still in her work clothes, press card swinging from its lanyard, leaned over his shoulder to read it a third time out loud.

"Provisionally selected," she read, "pending physical and medical examination." She looked at him. "Nanna's going to cry. Loudly. In front of everyone."

"He'll deny it afterward."

"He always does."

Down the corridor, Vashista's voice carried through from where he sat with the newspaper, half-listening the way fathers always half-listen. "What's that? Read it properly, don't mumble."

Sandhya grinned at Vivaswa, and read it properly.

Some wounds, Vivaswa had come to understand, never fully closed — Suresh's death still sat somewhere unresolved in him, a door he'd wanted to walk through and never got the chance to; Manish's paralysis was a fact he carried without displaying, the way he carried everything. Sandhya still, on quiet nights, asked him without warning whether he was certain, truly certain, that he didn't regret it, and he still answered the same way every time, because the answer hadn't changed.

But the letter sat on the table between them now, ordinary and official and entirely unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what it had cost to arrive at, and for the first time in longer than he could easily measure, Vivaswa found that the future in front of him took up more of his attention than the year behind him.

His father's voice called out again from the other room, impatient now. "Vivaswa! Are you coming to explain this or not?"

He folded the letter, slipped it into his pocket, and went.
