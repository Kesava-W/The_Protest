**Chapter 1: The Catalyst**

**Scene 1 – The Accident & The Observer**

*Residential streets, Hyderabad*

The morning traffic of Hyderabad moved with its usual rhythm — vendors arranging vegetables beside the road in neat pyramids, school buses crawling through narrow lanes with children's faces pressed to dusty windows, office-goers hurrying toward the nearest bus stop with one eye on their phones and the other on the traffic.

Vivaswa adjusted the strap of his faded laptop bag as he walked beside his father, Vashista, past the tea stall where the same three men argued about cricket every single morning.

"You'll be late," Vashista said with a smile.

"I still have fifteen minutes."

"You've been saying that since college."

Vivaswa smiled faintly. "I've never actually been late."

Vashista laughed, the sound easy and unhurried. "That's because you calculate everything."

They continued walking. A neighbourhood shopkeeper, already stacking soap bars into a pyramid display, looked up and greeted them.

"Good morning, Vashista garu."

Vashista stopped for a moment to ask about the man's recovering son — how the fever had broken, whether the medicines were helping, whether he needed anything. Even while speaking, his attention remained genuine. He never hurried conversations with people, no matter how late he himself might be running.

Vivaswa watched him quietly from a step behind. His father had an unusual ability to make people feel heard, as though the rest of the world could wait its turn.

As they reached the junction near the bus stop, someone called out.

"Vivaswa!"

He turned. It was Arun, an old colleague from MegaT, waving him over with the particular urgency of someone who wanted to gossip before the office day swallowed them both.

"Just two minutes."

Vashista waved casually, already a few steps ahead. "I'll walk ahead. Come."

"You go. I'll catch up."

Arun fell into step beside him instead, asking about work. "The monthly reports finally finished?"

"Yesterday."

"Still the top performer?"

Vivaswa shrugged. "They say numbers don't lie."

Arun laughed. "They also don't get promotions."

They exchanged a few more words — nothing important, the kind of small talk that fills the gap between two people who used to sit three desks apart. Then, behind them, somewhere past the junction —

A sharp screech. Tyres screamed against the road. A heavy thud rolled through the street like something physical, something that pressed against the chest.

People gasped. Someone shouted, "Accident!"

Vivaswa turned.

His father lay motionless on the road. A white SUV was already pulling away, its engine note rising as it gathered speed.

Time did something strange then. It didn't stop — nothing so dramatic — it simply thinned, the way it does in the half-second before a fall, giving him more of it to work with than he should have had.

Instead of running immediately, something instinctive took over. His eyes followed the vehicle rather than his father.

White SUV. Recent model. Front bumper dented on the left corner. A black sticker, oval, on the rear windshield. Partial registration — *TS09…K…7* — the rest lost behind a cloud of exhaust. The driver: male, dark beard, gripping the wheel with both hands. The passenger beside him: clean-shaven, blue checked shirt, turning once to look back through the rear window before the vehicle swung left at the next intersection and was gone.

Only then did Vivaswa run.

"Dad!"

He dropped to his knees beside Vashista. Blood trickled from a cut above his father's eyebrow, thin and dark against the grey of the road. His left leg lay twisted at an angle that made Vivaswa's stomach turn.

"Call an ambulance!" someone yelled. Another person was already dialling 108, phone pressed hard against their ear.

Vivaswa gently supported his father's head, feeling the tremor in his own hands for the first time.

"Dad…"

Vashista's eyes opened slowly, unfocused for a moment before finding his son's face.

"I'm alright…"

"You don't have to speak."

A weak smile flickered across Vashista's lips despite everything. "I told you… you'd still reach before me."

Even now, bleeding on a public road with his leg bent wrong, he was trying to reassure his son. Vivaswa gripped his father's hand tightly, as though holding on hard enough could keep him from slipping further away.

The ambulance siren grew louder in the distance. Around them, people argued about whose fault it was, some already chasing the disappearing vehicle on foot for no reason except that running felt better than standing still, others recording videos on their phones for reasons Vivaswa didn't care to examine.

He ignored every voice. His eyes remained fixed on his father's face.

And yet — somewhere beneath the fear, in a part of his mind that refused to switch off even now — every detail of the fleeing SUV sat perfectly intact, filed away like evidence he hadn't yet decided what to do with.

He knew, with a strange and unwelcome certainty, that he would never forget them.

**Scene 2 – The Hospital & The Chemist**

The rhythmic beeping of a cardiac monitor filled the small hospital room, steady enough to become invisible after the first hour.

Vivaswa sat beside the bed, elbows resting on his knees. His father's left leg, encased in a thick white plaster cast, lay elevated on a pillow. The smell of antiseptic hung in the air, mixing with the faint chemical sweetness of freshly mopped floors and, underneath it, the particular hush that hospital corridors carry regardless of the hour.

Vashista looked toward the window, where the afternoon sun filtered gold through thin curtains.

"So…" he said, smiling faintly, "…looks like your old man has finally become a burden."

Vivaswa looked up immediately. "Don't say that."

"I was joking."

"I wasn't."

For a moment, neither of them spoke. The doctor's words from half an hour earlier still echoed in Vivaswa's mind, arranged in the clipped, careful order doctors use when they are about to say something difficult.

*Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma. Aggressive. Treatable. But treatment had to begin quickly.*

He had understood every word. What he hadn't understood — what no one had bothered to explain, because it wasn't a medical question — was how he was going to pay for it.

The door opened. A man in his early thirties entered, carrying a small paper bag from a nearby pharmacy, his shirt still smelling faintly of the two-wheeler ride over.

"Madhav," Vivaswa said, standing.

Madhav smiled warmly. "How is Uncle?"

"The pain is manageable," Vashista answered before Vivaswa could. "I've survived worse."

Madhav chuckled, setting the bag on the side table. "You've been saying that since we were kids." He handed it to Vivaswa. "I checked every distributor in the city. Most of these medicines are available immediately. A couple aren't — I've already placed requests with two wholesalers."

Vivaswa looked inside. Every strip was neatly labelled, some with handwritten instructions attached in Madhav's cramped handwriting. *Morning. After food. Do not skip.*

"You didn't have to do all this," Vivaswa said quietly.

Madhav shrugged, the gesture of a man who had clearly already had this argument in his head and won it. "You'd have done the same."

Vashista smiled. "I keep telling him he chose the wrong profession."

"No, Uncle. Pharmacy pays the bills." Madhav's joke lightened the room for a few seconds — only a few.

Vivaswa looked at the prescription again, folding it along its existing crease rather than making a new one. "So these are enough for now?"

"For now."

"And the rest?"

"They'll arrive in a day or two." Madhav paused, choosing his next words more carefully than the ones before. "The chemotherapy drugs will be the expensive part."

Silence returned, heavier this time. Vivaswa folded the prescription with unnecessary precision, as though careful hands could somehow make the numbers smaller.

"I'll manage."

Madhav looked at him steadily. "You don't have to pretend with me."

"I wasn't pretending."

"You were." A pause. "If you need money—"

"I'll ask."

"You promise?"

"…Yes."

Madhav nodded, satisfied enough to let the subject go for now, and turned to Vashista instead. "Doctor says you'll be walking after the fracture heals."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"I still have to beat him in our evening walks." Vashista pointed a thumb at his son. "He thinks he'll finally win."

Madhav laughed. "I don't think anyone has ever beaten Uncle."

"Exactly."

Even Vivaswa managed a small smile — it disappeared as quickly as it had come, and Madhav noticed but chose not to comment. Instead, he reached into his pocket and pulled out a folded slip of paper.

"I've written my number for the oncology department pharmacist. If any medicine isn't available here, call me before buying elsewhere. I'll check every supplier first."

Vivaswa accepted it. "Thanks."

"No thanks between friends." Madhav placed a reassuring hand on his shoulder. "We'll get through this."

After a brief conversation with Vashista — mostly Vashista insisting he was fine, and Madhav pretending to believe him — Madhav excused himself and left. The room settled back into its quiet rhythm. Outside, a wheelchair rolled past, a nurse called out a patient's name, and somewhere down the corridor a child cried before being hushed.

Vashista watched his son staring at the folded prescription. "You've become very quiet."

"I'm thinking."

"About money?"

Vivaswa didn't answer. His father sighed and, after a moment, said, "Listen to me carefully."

Vivaswa finally looked up.

"I've lived a good life. If God gives me more years, I'll gladly accept them." Vashista smiled gently. "If He doesn't, don't destroy yours trying to save mine."

Vivaswa held his father's gaze without blinking. "I wasn't raised to give up."

"I know."

"And I won't start now."

For the first time since the accident, something like uncertainty flickered across Vashista's face — not fear exactly, but the recognition of a tone he knew too well. When Vivaswa said something in that particular voice, low and final, he rarely changed his mind afterward.

The monitor continued its steady rhythm. Neither of them spoke again. Only the prescription remained in Vivaswa's hand, folded so neatly that not a single corner was bent.

**Scene 3 – The Layoff & The Analyst**

The office floor of MegaT was unusually quiet that morning. Rows of employees stared into their monitors, fingers tapping keyboards with mechanical rhythm, the central air-conditioning humming softly above them. Nothing looked different. And yet something felt wrong, the way a room feels wrong the moment before someone in it starts crying.

Vivaswa noticed it the instant he entered.

His team leader, Prakash, who usually greeted everyone with a cheerful nod and a half-joke about the weekend, merely glanced at him before looking away. Two colleagues abruptly stopped talking as he walked past, their conversation dying mid-sentence. Another avoided eye contact altogether, suddenly very interested in his own screen.

He took his seat without reacting and logged into his workstation. The dashboard loaded.

*Top Performer – Current Month.* His employee ID still occupied the first position. *Accuracy: 99.96%. Productivity: Highest in Team.*

He stared at the numbers for a second before returning to work, as though they might still matter in an hour.

Half an hour later, an email appeared. *Subject: HR Meeting – Immediate.* No explanation. No agenda. He quietly stood up.

The Human Resources cabin was small but spotless, the kind of room designed to make bad news feel procedural rather than personal. An HR executive sat across from him, Prakash beside her, both wearing expressions that looked rehearsed — the specific blankness of people who have delivered this same sentence to someone else earlier that week.

"Please have a seat, Vivaswa."

He sat. The HR executive folded her hands.

"I'm sorry to inform you that your position has been made redundant as part of organisational restructuring."

Silence. She slid an envelope across the table. "This contains your relieving documents and final settlement details."

Vivaswa looked at the envelope but didn't touch it. Instead, he observed the room. Prakash never once looked him in the eye. The HR executive had delivered the sentence almost word-for-word from memory, the cadence of someone reciting rather than explaining. Neither looked surprised by his lack of reaction — which meant, he understood, that they had expected either tears or an argument, and had been briefed on how to handle both.

"May I know why my role was selected?" he asked calmly.

"The decision was based on business requirements."

"I see." Nothing more.

As he stood to leave, fragments from the previous week began aligning themselves in his mind with unwelcome clarity. Prakash's unusually long meetings with Rohan, closed-door and frequent. The sudden praise Rohan had received despite repeatedly missing his targets. The Operations Manager requesting productivity reports from his team alone, out of the whole floor. The whispered conversation outside the conference room that had stopped abruptly the moment he approached it last Thursday.

The restructuring had not begun today. It had begun days earlier, quietly, in rooms he hadn't been invited into. No one had questioned his performance. They had simply decided someone else would stay.

Office politics. He understood it now, all of it, in the particular way understanding arrives too late to be useful. He also understood that arguing would change nothing.

He signed the documents. Returned his ID card. Collected the envelope. As he walked through the office for the last time, several colleagues looked uncomfortable — one gave him an apologetic smile, another lowered his head as though the floor had suddenly become interesting. Only Rohan appeared unusually relaxed, leaning back in his chair with the ease of a man who had already been told the good news.

Vivaswa noticed. He remembered. But he said nothing.

Outside the building, the afternoon heat struck his face like a physical thing. Employees hurried past with coffee cups, office bags, phone calls trailing behind them. Life continued exactly as before, indifferent to the fact that his had just changed.

He looked once at the MegaT logo fixed above the entrance. A week ago, losing this job would have been painful. Today, it felt terrifying. His father had cancer. The hospital bills had only begun. His savings would not last long.

For the first time since the accident, uncertainty outweighed grief.

He folded the envelope, slipped it into his bag, and began walking home — not because he had accepted what had happened, but because he had no other choice.

**Scene 4 – The Breakdown & The Spark**

The staircase landing between the fourth and fifth floors was unusually quiet, holding only the faint hum of the lift motor and, drifting in through the open corridor, the distant barking of stray dogs settling in for the evening.

Vivaswa climbed the stairs slowly. The envelope containing his termination letter felt heavier than paper had any right to feel. *Top Performer. Outstanding Accuracy. Reliable Employee.* None of it had mattered.

He unlocked the apartment door halfway before stopping. Inside, his father was asleep after another exhausting day of hospital visits, medicines, and uncertainty. Vivaswa quietly closed the door again. He couldn't let Vashista see him like this.

Without quite deciding to, he walked back down and sat on the cold concrete steps between the floors. For several minutes he stared blankly at the envelope in his hands, his fingers tightening around it until the paper crumpled beneath them. The medical reports flashed through his mind — *Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma. Chemotherapy. Targeted therapy.* Costs he could no longer imagine paying, not even in the vague, deferred way people usually avoid imagining such things. His salary had barely kept them afloat. Now even that was gone.

For the first time since the accident, his composure broke. He lowered his head into his hands. The tears came silently — no shouting, no anger, just complete helplessness, the kind that doesn't announce itself with noise.

"Vivaswa?"

He didn't respond. A softer voice followed. "Vivaswa…"

He looked up. Sandhya stood a few steps above him, a laptop bag over one shoulder, probably just back from work. Her smile disappeared the moment she saw his face. Without asking permission, she sat beside him. Neither of them spoke. She waited, knowing instinctively that forcing words out of him would only make things harder.

After nearly a minute, Vivaswa finally spoke. "I lost my job." A pause. "They laid me off today."

Another long silence. "I don't understand. I was the top performer. I never missed deadlines. I never argued with anyone." He gave a hollow laugh. "Apparently that wasn't enough."

Sandhya gently took the crumpled envelope from his hand, glanced at it, and folded it carefully — the way you'd fold something you intended to keep. "Is that all?"

He shook his head. "No. My father—" His voice cracked. "The doctors confirmed it." He swallowed hard. "It's Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma."

Sandhya froze.

"He'll need treatment. A lot of it. I don't even know where the money will come from now."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Then Sandhya quietly placed her hand over his. "It'll be alright."

Vivaswa shook his head. "No. It won't. I couldn't protect him. I couldn't stop the accident. And now… I can't even pay to save him." His shoulders trembled.

Sandhya had never seen him like this — the calm, measured man who noticed everything before he ever spoke, undone on a staircase with a crumpled envelope in his lap. She remembered teasing him about that quality once, and even now, despite everything, she managed a faint smile.

"You know…" she said softly, "you notice everything."

Vivaswa looked at her. His lips formed the faintest smile. "Only the things worth remembering."

The words lingered between them longer than either intended. Sandhya realised that even now, in the middle of his own collapse, he hadn't forgotten a single detail of the accident — not the car, not the faces, not the direction it had fled. Not anything. She had admired that quality long before she knew he loved her. Now, she thought she finally understood why.

She took a slow breath. "I'm tired of pretending."

Vivaswa frowned slightly. "What?"

"I know. You… love me."

He stared at her. Before he could deny it, she continued.

"Rohit told me. I've known for months."

Embarrassment flashed across his face. "I never wanted you to know like that."

"I wasn't going to tell you."

"Why?"

He looked away. "What would I tell you? I earn just enough for us to survive. My father is fighting cancer. I don't even have a job anymore. I had no right to ask you to build a life around all this."

Sandhya felt her eyes sting. "You idiot." He looked at her, confused. "You decided all that… without asking me?"

She laughed softly through forming tears. "I fell in love with you long before Rohit told me anything. I kept waiting. I thought you'd tell me. You never did. So now I am." She moved closer. "I love you, Vivaswa."

The world around him seemed to stop. For several seconds he couldn't speak.

"You deserve someone better."

She smiled. "I'll decide that."

Before he could answer, she wrapped her arms around him. He hesitated only briefly before embracing her back, and the weight that had crushed him all evening seemed, for just a moment, lighter than it had any right to be.

Sandhya gently cupped his face. "Whatever comes next… we'll face it together."

She leaned forward and kissed him. It wasn't impulsive. It wasn't dramatic. It was a quiet promise — the kind made on an empty staircase between two people who had suddenly, unexpectedly, found something worth fighting for.

Neither of them noticed the figure standing at the turn of the staircase above.

Viswamithra had returned home earlier than usual. The grocery bag in his hand hung motionless as he watched his daughter embrace and kiss Vivaswa, his expression hardening with every second that passed. Without making a sound, he turned around and walked away.

The two below never knew he had been there. The silence he carried with him up the remaining flight of stairs would speak far louder, in the days to come, than anything he might have said.
