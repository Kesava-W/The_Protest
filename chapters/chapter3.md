**Chapter 3: The Crackdown**

**Scene 1 – The Silent Treatment**

*Viswamithra's house, Hyderabad. Early morning.*

The television in the living room ran a news bulletin. "...Police have received intelligence that a section of the protesters may attempt to march towards the Vidhan Sabha..." Visuals from the protest flashed across the screen, and among the crowd, Vivaswa appeared briefly before the footage moved on to something else.

Viswamithra, who had been sipping his morning tea, set the cup down on the table harder than he meant to.

"Sandhya!"

His voice carried through the house. She emerged from her room already dressed for work, a sling bag on her shoulder.

"What happened, Dad?"

He pointed at the television, then let out a short, humourless laugh. "This is what happened." The footage replayed. "There he is. Your Vivaswa. He has left his sick father at home and is standing in protests. What exactly is he trying to achieve?"

Sandhya remained quiet. Viswamithra frowned, unused to this particular silence from her.

"I warned you. A man who cannot secure his own future has no business trying to change the country's future." Still nothing. He had expected an argument — ordinarily she challenged him on almost everything, from politics to journalism to her own career choices — but today there was nothing to push against.

He continued anyway. "He lost his job. His father is battling cancer. And instead of finding another job, he has joined people shouting slogans on the road." He shook his head. "I expected better judgement from him."

Silence. He studied her face carefully. "Aren't you going to defend him?"

Sandhya slowly met his eyes. A hundred things passed through her mind in the space of that look — chief among them, a promise she'd made weeks earlier, to a man she trusted, about a silence that wasn't hers to break. She still didn't know everything Vivaswa was doing at that protest ground. She only knew it mattered enough that she'd agreed never to ask him about it where anyone could hear.

She looked away. "I don't have anything to say."

Viswamithra blinked, and the answer surprised him more than any argument could have. For years, his daughter had never once backed away from a debate. Today she simply absorbed every accusation without resistance — not agreeing, he realised as he studied her, but *enduring*, which was a different thing entirely and somehow more unsettling.

His anger slowly gave way to confusion. After a long pause he sighed. "I hope you know what you're doing."

Sandhya gave a faint nod. "So do I." She picked up her helmet from the table. "I'm leaving."

Without waiting for another question, she walked toward the door. Only after it closed behind her did she finally let out the breath she'd been holding. She hated lying to her father. But she'd given her word — to a man she trusted, and to Vivaswa — and for now, that word mattered more than anything her father needed to hear.

Outside, she pulled on her helmet, started her scooter, and rode toward the protest site — not as Vivaswa's lover, but as a journalist determined to uncover who was funding a movement that had begun as a protest and was slowly turning into something far more dangerous.

**Scene 2 – The Investigative Journalist**

The newsroom was unusually quiet for late afternoon, most reporters out chasing assignments, leaving only the soft hum of computers and the occasional ringing of an unanswered telephone.

Sandhya stared at the whiteboard beside her desk. At its centre, in bold letters: **E20 PROTEST**. Around it, arrows connected names, locations, question marks. One sticky note read *Funding?* Another, *Paid participants?* A third, *Political link?*

She leaned back in her chair. "Something doesn't fit."

The protest had started as a citizens' movement. But somewhere along the way it had changed — every day the crowds grew larger, every day new faces appeared, and yet many of them disappeared before sunset. It wasn't behaving like a normal public movement, and she had covered enough of them to know the difference in her gut before she could prove it on paper.

Her phone vibrated. "Madam, I'm outside." One of her local stringers.

She went downstairs. The man handed her a small notebook. "I spoke to three shopkeepers near the protest ground." She opened it, and one line immediately caught her eye: *Every morning, a few people arrive in vans. They don't shout slogans until someone tells them to.* She underlined it.

"What about food?"

"They're getting packets twice a day."

"Who distributes them?"

He shook his head. "Couldn't identify him."

"What about money?"

"I couldn't see any direct payments."

Sandhya frowned. No confirmation — only suspicion, and suspicion wasn't enough. She closed the notebook. "Good work. Don't assume anything. We need evidence, not rumours."

The stringer nodded and left.

An hour later, she sat inside a small tea stall overlooking one side of the protest area, not as Vivaswa's neighbour and not because of any personal feeling, but because she was, first and always, a journalist. She watched quietly. Some protesters passionately argued about fuel prices. Others discussed agricultural costs. Several families had come carrying handmade placards, and they looked entirely genuine. But scattered among them were men who seemed strangely detached — no slogans, no discussions, simply waiting, watching, following instructions that arrived by some signal she couldn't yet see.

She made another note. *Mixed crowd. Genuine participants + organised elements.* She refused to paint everyone with the same brush. That would be dishonest, and dishonesty in her notebook had a way of becoming dishonesty in print.

A police vehicle rolled past slowly. An officer she recognised stepped out. "Still working?" he asked, smiling.

She smiled back politely. "Trying to understand who's really behind this."

He shrugged. "If you find out, let us know."

"If I can prove it," she said, laughing lightly, "it'll become tomorrow's headline."

He smiled and drove off. Neither had revealed anything confidential — both understood the line, and both respected it, which was more than she could say for half the officers she'd dealt with over the years.

As evening approached, she packed her notebook and looked once more toward the gathering. Some people had clearly come to protest. Some had clearly come for something else. The difficult part, always, was separating one from the other — and that, she reminded herself, was where journalism mattered most: not amplifying rumours, not dismissing legitimate grievances, only finding facts. Facts, and nothing else.

She walked away unaware that events were already moving toward a confrontation neither the protesters nor the police could now avoid.

**Scene 3 – The Riot & The Trap**

The afternoon sun bore down relentlessly over the designated protest ground. Sweat soaked shirts and banners alike as hundreds of protesters continued chanting against E20 petrol — some out of conviction, others because someone had paid them, the two motives standing shoulder to shoulder without either one able to tell the other apart.

Vivaswa stood near the middle of the gathering, watching. Not shouting. Not leading. Watching. His eyes travelled from face to face out of habit now more than intention — the elderly farmer who'd been present almost every single day; the college students arguing among themselves about something that had nothing to do with fuel prices; the labourers quietly discussing returning to work after sunset. And then, the familiar group — the same men who never raised a slogan unless someone instructed them to, who kept glancing toward Manish for signals.

One of them discreetly checked his phone. A message arrived. He nodded. Vivaswa noticed, and said nothing.

A few metres away, Manish slowly sat up from his makeshift bed. His theatrical fast-unto-death had entered its final week, and cameras immediately turned toward him.

"Today we march to the Vidhan Sabha!"

The crowd erupted. Many of the genuine protesters looked startled. A middle-aged schoolteacher frowned. "What? Nobody said we'd march. We only came for the demonstration." Several others exchanged uneasy glances, murmuring among themselves.

Suresh climbed quickly onto the temporary stage. "This government won't listen unless we force them to!" The paid men cheered immediately; the genuine protesters hesitated. The atmosphere in the crowd shifted, the way a room's temperature drops before a storm.

Nearly two kilometres away, police wireless sets crackled continuously. "All units in position." "Barricades ready." "North approach secured." "South approach secured." Police vans quietly surrounded every possible entry point leading toward the Vidhan Sabha, riot-control personnel waiting behind layered barricades. No one advanced. They simply waited, exactly as planned.

The procession began moving — ordinary citizens still leading the front rows, the organised group walking behind them. Vivaswa remained somewhere in the middle, neither leading nor resisting, only observing.

As they approached the final barricade, a police officer raised a loudspeaker. "This is a prohibited zone. Please stop where you are. You may continue your protest at the designated location." For a few moments, nothing happened. The genuine protesters slowed; some even began turning back.

Then a stone flew through the air and struck a police shield with a loud crack. Silence. Another stone. Then another. Within seconds, half a dozen rocks rained toward the barricades, and the ordinary protesters looked around in confusion — "Who threw that?" "Stop!" "What are you doing?" — while the men throwing them ignored every voice but their own instructions.

One constable took a cut above his eyebrow. Another stumbled after being struck on the shoulder. The commanding officer raised his hand. "Tear gas." The order travelled instantly. Launchers fired, canisters arced through the sky and landed across the advancing crowd, and thick white smoke rapidly spread. Eyes burned. People coughed violently. Panic replaced slogans, and the genuine protesters scattered in every direction — some helping elderly participants escape, others simply running.

The stone-pelting group, meanwhile, remained visible despite the chaos. Police teams had already memorised their locations — not because of luck, but because they already knew exactly who they were. Weeks of CCTV footage. Weeks of intelligence. Weeks of reports from a trusted informer. Everything had been pieced together long before today; the violence merely confirmed it.

Within minutes, special teams moved in — not chasing everyone at random, but heading directly toward specific individuals. One man attempting to flee was intercepted instantly. Another tried removing his scarf, too late; his face had already been identified. A third attempted to blend into the fleeing crowd, and an officer called out his name across the chaos. He froze. One after another, the organisers of the violence disappeared into police vehicles.

Vivaswa quietly walked away with hundreds of ordinary protesters. A young constable stopped him briefly. "Name?" He gave it calmly. The constable checked a list on his tablet, nodded after a moment. "You're free to leave." Vivaswa thanked him and continued walking, sirens echoing through the evening behind him.

The protest was over.
