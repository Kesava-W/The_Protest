**Chapter 5: The Confession**

**Scene 1 – The Departure**

The hospital room fell silent after ACP Satvik finished speaking. He glanced once at Vashista, resting peacefully on the bed, the cardiac monitor filling the room with its soft electronic rhythm. Satisfied that everything was in order, he turned toward Vivaswa.

"I'll take your leave now."

Vivaswa rose from the chair. "Thank you, sir."

Satvik gave a small nod. "You've done well." His eyes shifted briefly to Sandhya. "Take care of them."

"I will," she replied.

He walked to the door, then paused. "And Vivaswa — when the recruitment notification comes out, apply. I'll recommend your name. The rest will depend on your own merit."

A faint smile appeared on Vivaswa's face. "I won't disappoint you, sir."

"I know."

Without another word, Satvik stepped outside. The door clicked shut behind him.

Silence settled over the room, broken only by the distant sound of trolley wheels echoing through the corridor, a nurse calling out a patient's name somewhere down the hall, a child laughing briefly before being hushed by his mother. Inside, none of them spoke. Vashista slept peacefully, exhausted after the day's therapy. Vivaswa sat back down, eyes fixed on the floor. Sandhya stood near the window, watching the lights of Hyderabad flicker beyond the hospital compound.

For the first time that evening, there was nothing left for either of them to say — at least, nothing easy. The biggest mysteries had already been answered. The men responsible for Vashista's accident had been identified. The protest had ended. Satvik had left. And yet an inexplicable heaviness lingered in the room, thick enough that Sandhya could feel it pressing against her chest.

She turned toward Vivaswa. He wasn't relieved. He wasn't satisfied. He was staring into empty space, as though carrying a burden he had never intended to share with anyone.

She noticed his fingers, tightly interlocked, knuckles gone pale. He had done that before — always when something weighed heavily on his mind. She took a few slow steps toward him, stopped beside the chair, and gently placed her hand over his. His fingers relaxed instinctively beneath her touch.

She searched his face. "What is it?"

He didn't answer. The silence stretched between them once more. Then, very slowly, he raised his eyes to meet hers. There was no anger in them. No fear. Only the quiet exhaustion of a man who had carried a secret for far too long.

Sandhya felt a knot form in her stomach. Whatever he was about to say, it was something he had never told another living soul. She waited — without interrupting, without looking away.

**Scene 2 – The Unvarnished Truth**

The soft hiss of the central air-conditioning blended with the distant beeping of monitors. Nurses walked past the corridor without paying attention to the two people standing near the window.

Sandhya had still not spoken. Vivaswa kept looking through the glass panel into his father's room, where Vashista had fallen asleep after the therapy session, his breathing calm for the first time that day.

"I poisoned him."

The words came without warning.

Sandhya slowly turned toward him. "What?"

"I poisoned Manish."

She stared at him, convinced she had misheard. "What did you just say?"

Vivaswa didn't flinch. "I poisoned Manish."

Silence. Not a trace of pride. Not a trace of hesitation. Only a plain statement, delivered the way he might have reported an update to Satvik.

Sandhya searched his face, hoping to find some sign that this was a cruel joke. Instead she found complete seriousness.

"…How?"

He looked back toward the hospital room before answering. "You remember I told you I kept observing everyone in the protest?"

She nodded slowly.

"I wasn't only observing them for Satvik sir." His voice remained calm. "I learnt Manish was asthmatic. He carried only one inhaler. I watched his routine for days — where he kept it, when he left it unattended, who went near it."

As he spoke, the memory returned to him whole, the way it always did — not in fragments, but in one continuous, unhurried sequence, exactly as it had happened. The floodlit camp after sunset. The argument near the entrance that had pulled half the ground toward the gate. *Come quickly! They're arguing with the police again!* The space around Manish's sleeping mat, emptied in seconds. He had counted to fifteen before he stood, stretched like a man whose legs had gone numb, and walked toward the water drums — past the bedding, past the unattended backpack. His hand had brushed against it in passing, one smooth motion, no hesitation, no glance behind. The old inhaler had disappeared into his sleeve. An identical one had taken its place before his next footstep landed.

"One night, when nobody was watching, I exchanged it with an identical inhaler." Sandhya's heartbeat quickened. She felt the blood drain from her face. "It was laced with arsenic."

The corridor suddenly felt colder.

"You…" She struggled to speak. "You… poisoned him… through his inhaler?"

Vivaswa nodded once. "It was only one swap. I knew he'd keep using the same inhaler for the rest of the protest. The poison worked slowly."

Sandhya took an involuntary step backwards, her mind racing through everything she knew about him — the quiet neighbour, the caring son, the man who massaged his father's feet every evening, the man who had cried when he lost his job. None of it matched the person standing before her now.

"You planned this?"

"For almost a month."

She covered her mouth. "My God…"

"I asked Madhav to prepare it."

She looked up instantly. "Madhav?"

"He helped prepare it."

That memory, too, came back to him whole. The pharmacy after closing, shutters half down, the smell of antiseptic and old paper. He'd asked Madhav to sit before he said anything, and Madhav — reading something in his friend's face he'd never seen there before — had sat without asking why.

"I need something," Vivaswa had said. "Something that won't be traced easily. Something slow."

Madhav had gone very still. "For what?"

"For the men who hit my father and drove away."

For a long moment, Madhav hadn't said anything at all. Then, quietly: "You understand what happens to you if this goes wrong."

"I do."

"Vivaswa—"

"I'm asking you once. I won't ask again, whatever you decide."

Madhav had looked at him for a long time — the look of a man weighing thirty years of friendship against everything he'd been raised to believe was right, and finding, in the end, that the friendship weighed more. He hadn't smiled. He hadn't agreed easily. But he had, eventually, nodded once, the way a man nods at a debt he already knows he'll have to pay.

"I'm doing this for your father," Madhav had said. "Not for you. Not for revenge. For him."

Vivaswa had understood, even then, exactly what that distinction cost his friend to make.

He offered Sandhya nothing more now than he'd offered Madhav that night — no excuses, no attempt to justify himself. Just facts, laid out the way he laid out everything.

Sandhya's breathing turned uneven. Every revelation felt heavier than the last. The image of Manish lying paralysed in a hospital bed flashed before her eyes, and then another replaced it — Vashista lying unconscious after the accident. Her thoughts collided; she couldn't process either one.

Without saying another word, she turned away. Her footsteps echoed through the corridor as she walked several metres and stopped near a window, resting both hands on the railing. The city lights shimmered outside.

Behind her, Vivaswa remained exactly where he was. He didn't call out. He didn't follow. He simply waited, and the silence between them grew heavier than any argument could have been.

**Scene 3 – The Confrontation**

The hospital corridor had fallen unusually quiet, holding only the rhythmic beeping of distant monitors, the squeak of a passing trolley, the low murmur of nurses somewhere out of sight.

Vivaswa remained standing near the window, his eyes fixed on the darkness outside. He heard footsteps — slow, measured. Sandhya stopped a few feet behind him.

For several seconds, neither of them spoke. Finally—

"What were you thinking?"

Her voice was low. Not loud. Not emotional. Just shaken.

Vivaswa didn't turn around. "I was thinking about my father."

Sandhya closed her eyes. "You poisoned him."

"I did."

"You planned it."

"Yes."

"You watched him inhale poison every single day, and said nothing." He answered before she could finish the sentence.

Silence. Sandhya took a deep breath, trying to steady herself. Then, suddenly, she walked in front of him.

"What if someone had seen you? What if someone recognised the inhaler? What if Manish had become suspicious? What if Madhav had told someone? What if the police had found out? What if Satvik sir had found out?" She wasn't crying — she was angry, terrified. "You think this ends with prison? You poisoned a man. If this had come out, your life would've been over. You would've spent decades in jail." She took another breath. "And if those people had realised you were the informer—" She couldn't finish the sentence.

Vivaswa quietly completed it. "They would've killed me."

"Exactly!" Her voice echoed through the corridor. "You knew that! You knew every risk!"

"I thought about every one of those possibilities," he said quietly. "Every night. And I still went ahead."

Sandhya stared at him in disbelief. "Why? You were already helping the police. Satvik sir was handling the case. The people responsible were eventually going to be found. So why?"

For the first time since his confession, Vivaswa looked tired — not guilty, just exhausted. His eyes wandered toward his father's room, where Vashista slept on, unaware.

"When they hit him," he said quietly, "I watched him lying on the road. I watched people gather. I watched strangers call an ambulance. I watched doctors tell me he had cancer. I watched him smile — so I wouldn't cry." His voice remained steady, and that steadiness frightened Sandhya more than any tremor could have. "I couldn't do anything. I couldn't stop the accident. I couldn't stop the cancer. I couldn't even keep my job. I was watching everything happen, and doing nothing." He paused. "When Satvik sir asked me to become an informer, I agreed because it was the right thing. I wanted those people arrested. I wanted the protest network destroyed. I don't regret that." He looked directly into her eyes. "But that wasn't enough."

Sandhya listened without interrupting.

"They drove away. They left my father bleeding on the road. As if his life meant nothing." His jaw tightened. "And the men who did it stood on a stage afterward. Giving speeches. Acting like leaders. I couldn't accept that."

"I wasn't trying to send a message. I wasn't trying to change society. I wasn't trying to punish corruption. This wasn't about the police. It wasn't about the courts. It wasn't about justice." He looked directly into her eyes. "It was personal. I wanted the man who crippled my father to lose the life he had taken for granted. I never wanted revenge against the law. I never blamed the police — they were doing their job. So was I." He exhaled slowly. "My revenge was mine. It wasn't anyone else's."

The words hung heavily between them. Sandhya's anger hadn't disappeared — if anything, hearing his reasoning made the weight of what he had done feel even greater. She stepped closer.

"You could have lost everything. My father. Our future." She paused. "Me."

That single word struck him harder than any accusation. For the first time since Satvik had left, Vivaswa looked away.

"I know." His voice was almost a whisper.

Sandhya searched his face. "Do you regret it?"

The question lingered. After a long silence—

"No." There was no hesitation. No pride. No satisfaction. Just certainty. "I regret the risk. I regret what it would have done to all of you if I had failed. I regret putting you through this." He shook his head slowly. "But not the revenge. If I hadn't done it, I don't think I would ever have found peace. I loved my father too much. I know what that says about me. I'll live with it. I already am."

Sandhya stood motionless. She didn't agree. She couldn't. But for the first time, she fully understood the depth of the wound that had driven him there.

The corridor fell silent again. Neither of them noticed the nurse walking past, or the elevator doors opening somewhere down the hall. She wasn't hearing the words anymore — she was looking at the man standing before her. The same gentle son who spent hours massaging his father's feet. The same man who had quietly become a police informer. The same man who had patiently planned a murder that never quite became one.

She realised something unsettling. Neither version of him was false. Both were real. And that frightened her more than the confession itself.

**Scene 4 – The Nuanced Acceptance**

The silence lingered. Neither of them spoke. Outside the hospital room a wheelchair rolled past; somewhere down the corridor a cardiac monitor beeped steadily; nurses walked by, exchanging hushed conversations before disappearing around the corner.

Sandhya stood near the window, her back to Vivaswa. She had heard everything — the confession, the reason, the certainty in his voice. She closed her eyes.

"I still hate what you did." Her voice was calm now. Not angry. Not trembling. Just tired.

Vivaswa lowered his head. "I know."

Another silence settled between them.

"I don't think I ever can accept poisoning someone."

"I don't expect you to."

Sandhya turned toward him slowly. His face held neither satisfaction nor guilt. Only peace. A peace that had come at a terrible price.

She thought, unexpectedly, of every story she had ever chased that had gone nowhere — every corrupt contractor who kept his license, every man who paid his way out of an FIR, every protest leader who took money to manufacture outrage and walked free while genuine grievances were drowned out underneath it. She had spent years writing the truth down and watching it change nothing. Vivaswa hadn't waited for the system to decide whether Manish deserved consequences. He had simply made sure there were some.

It didn't make what he'd done right. She wasn't going to tell herself that, not tonight and probably not ever. But some small, professional part of her — the part that had sat across from too many men who'd gotten away with far worse — understood the impulse in a way she wasn't proud of and couldn't fully argue against either.

She walked back toward him. For a long moment she simply looked into his eyes.

"I understand why you did it." She took a deep breath. "But promise me one thing."

He nodded.

"If life ever pushes you into darkness again, don't walk into it alone. Come to me first. I may not always agree with you. I may even fight with you." A faint smile appeared through her moist eyes. "But don't ever leave me outside your battles."

Vivaswa looked at her silently, then nodded. "I promise."

She stepped closer. "I was never afraid that you loved your father too much. I was afraid that I would lose you."

He reached for her hand. This time she did not hesitate. Their fingers intertwined, and the tension that had filled the room since Satvik's departure slowly began to dissolve.

After a while, Sandhya smiled. "There is one more thing."

Vivaswa looked up.

"I finally convinced Dad." He blinked. "He knows why you joined the protest. He knows you were helping the police. He knows you weren't one of those criminals." She laughed softly. "It took a lot of arguments. And a lot of shouting. But he finally said—" she imitated her father's stern voice, "—'If the boy has the courage to stand for what's right, and the sense to help the police instead of those criminals, then I have no objection.'"

For the first time since the conversation began, Vivaswa smiled — a genuine one. "I wasn't expecting that."

"Neither was I."

They both laughed quietly. The heaviness of the previous hour hadn't disappeared. It never would. Some wounds did not heal. Some choices could never be undone. But life, somehow, still moved forward.

Vivaswa looked through the hospital window. His father slept peacefully. For the first time in months, the future no longer looked empty.

Sandhya gently rested her head against his shoulder. Neither of them spoke again.

They didn't need to.

The corridor outside remained busy. Inside the room, there was finally peace.
