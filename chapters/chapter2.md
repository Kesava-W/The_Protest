**Chapter 2: The Infiltration**

**Scene 1 – The Protest Ecosystem**

The designated protest ground had been set up on an open stretch of land, fenced off with metal barricades by the city administration. Colourful banners demanding the rollback of E20 petrol fluttered under the afternoon sun. Slogans echoed rhythmically through loudspeakers, occasionally drowned out by the blaring horns from the nearby road, where traffic slowed only enough to gawk before moving on.

Some protesters shouted with genuine conviction. Some merely copied the slogans after hearing others, a beat behind, like a chorus still learning the song. Some looked more interested in the food packets than in the speeches.

Vivaswa walked into the crowd, a plain backpack slung over one shoulder, dressed in an ordinary shirt and faded jeans that blended him in without effort. Nobody noticed him. Exactly the way he wanted it.

He took a slow look around. A temporary stage stood at one end, large flex banners displaying photographs of the protest organisers above it. Near the stage, television cameras focused only on whoever stood before the microphones. Away from the cameras, reality looked different — a group of young men laughed loudly while taking selfies against the banners; another group argued in low voices about the daily allowance they'd been promised; an elderly farmer sat silently beneath a tree, wiping sweat from his forehead, listening to every speech with an attention the others had long since stopped affording it.

Vivaswa noticed the difference. *Not everyone here has come for the same reason.*

His eyes kept moving — not watching the speeches, but watching people. Who arrived together. Who never mixed with the others. Who remained unusually alert while everyone around them relaxed. Who seemed to be giving instructions rather than receiving them.

At the centre of the gathering stood Manish — thin, unshaven, wrapped in a white shawl, lying motionless on a mattress under a shamiana, surrounded by supporters. A large banner behind him declared: **FAST UNTO DEATH**. Television reporters eagerly captured every frame; people folded their hands before him as though visiting a saint.

Vivaswa observed quietly. *Too many cameras. A carefully chosen position. A doctor checks him every few hours.* Interesting.

And underneath that clinical read, something colder, something that had nothing to do with cameras or staging. He knew that face. Three weeks had roughened it — a beard grown out for sympathy rather than neglect, the haggard look of a man performing suffering for an audience — but the eyes hadn't changed, and neither had the particular way they'd once turned to glance back through a rear windshield before a white SUV swung left and disappeared. Vivaswa's expression didn't move. He simply filed it away, exactly as he'd filed away everything else that morning weeks ago, and kept walking his eyes across the crowd as though he'd noticed nothing at all.

His gaze shifted. A few metres away stood Suresh, and unlike Manish, Suresh never stayed in one place — moving constantly, speaking to volunteers, directing newcomers, talking to media personnel, answering phone calls that seemed to arrive with suspicious regularity. Where Manish attracted sympathy, Suresh appeared to be managing the machinery behind it. Vivaswa stored the observation away. Nothing more. Nothing less. Except that it wasn't nothing — he'd seen that face too, for less than two seconds, gripping a steering wheel as the vehicle pulled away from his father's body. He watched Suresh climb onto a stage weeks later, shake hands, smile for a passing camera, and did not, for even a fraction of a second, allow his own face to show that he already knew exactly who he was looking at.

As the day progressed, he began recognising different groups within the protest: genuine protesters who passionately debated fuel policy; college students who seemed more interested in the social media coverage than the cause itself; daily-wage labourers who waited quietly near the food distribution area, patient in the particular way of people used to waiting. And there were a handful of men who spoke very little — yet everyone else seemed careful not to cross them. That interested him most of all.

One of them briefly glanced his way. Vivaswa responded with the same indifferent expression as everyone else around him, and the man looked away, satisfied. Good. Invisible again.

The slogans grew louder — "Rollback E20!", "Save the common man!" — and the crowd repeated them enthusiastically. Vivaswa joined the chant, not because he believed shouting would change anything, but because people who never shouted were the ones who got remembered. He preferred not to be remembered.

As evening approached, volunteers began distributing drinking water and food packets. Vivaswa accepted one politely. A middle-aged man beside him smiled. "First day?"

He nodded.

"Don't worry. You'll get used to this."

"I hope so," Vivaswa said, with a faint smile that cost him nothing.

He looked once more across the gathering — the speeches, the cameras, the genuine anger, the opportunists, the disciplined men who watched everything without seeming to watch anything at all. Every protest had a surface. Every protest had a structure beneath it. He intended to understand both, and he had all the patience in the world to do it.

**Scene 2 – Identifying Targets & Routines**

The protest had entered its fourth day. The initial enthusiasm had faded — television cameras no longer arrived every morning, and the slogans had become routine, shouted more out of habit than conviction, the way a prayer gets recited without anyone quite listening to the words anymore.

Vivaswa quietly observed. He wasn't among those trying to lead slogans. He wasn't trying to impress anyone. He simply watched, one by one — every face, every routine, every pattern.

A white SUV stopped outside the barricades. It wasn't unusual; several vehicles came and went every day. But this one caught his attention — not because of the vehicle, but because of the man waiting for it.

Suresh had been chatting with protesters moments earlier. The instant the SUV stopped, he walked away casually, as though answering a phone call, and disappeared behind a temporary stage erected near the edge of the site. No announcement. No explanation. Nobody else seemed interested.

Vivaswa drifted toward a nearby tea stall without appearing to follow anyone. From there he could barely see behind the stage — enough to watch a man in sunglasses step out of the SUV. No introductions. No greetings. Just a thick brown envelope, changing hands.

Suresh opened it quickly. Bundles of cash, wrapped in bank paper bands, visible even from that distance. He counted nothing, simply slipping the envelope into a black backpack before the man returned to the SUV and it drove away. The entire exchange lasted less than thirty seconds.

Vivaswa sipped his tea without moving closer or looking again. He didn't need to. The faces had already been committed to memory.

As Suresh walked back, his expression hadn't changed at all. He climbed onto the makeshift stage almost immediately. "Our movement cannot be bought!" The crowd applauded. Vivaswa said nothing, and drank the rest of his tea slowly.

A loud cough interrupted the speech — Manish, sitting on his mattress beneath the FAST UNTO DEATH banner, surrounded by supporters who folded their hands or touched his feet while journalists photographed him at intervals. He coughed again, harder this time. A volunteer hurried over with an inhaler; Manish took two puffs, and his breathing steadied. The volunteer placed the inhaler beside his pillow.

Ten minutes later, another group of visitors arrived, and the inhaler disappeared into Manish's shoulder bag. An hour later it returned to the mattress. Later that evening it rested beside a folded blanket. Nobody seemed to notice the small, unremarkable choreography of it.

Except Vivaswa.

The following morning, again — Manish reached for the same inhaler immediately after waking. Always before speaking. Always before shouting slogans. Always before facing the media. By the end of the week, Vivaswa no longer needed to consciously observe; the routine had become predictable enough to hold in the back of his mind without effort. The inhaler was always nearby. Manish trusted nobody else to carry it — except, for a few minutes every evening while he washed his face behind the temporary medical tent, when the bag sat unattended. Only for a short while. Then someone from his inner circle picked it up again.

Vivaswa silently filed the timing away. No emotion crossed his face — not because he was suppressing one, but because there was, for the moment, nothing to suppress. Only arithmetic.

Near sunset, another protester sat beside him. "You don't talk much."

"I prefer listening," Vivaswa said, smiling politely.

The man chuckled. "First protest?"

"Yes."

"I've been coming to these things for years." The man leaned back, comfortable. Vivaswa looked at him.

"You believe in this cause?"

The man shrugged. "I believe in feeding my family." He smiled without embarrassment, leaning closer. "They paid me two thousand rupees. I come when they call." He stood and walked away before Vivaswa could respond, unbothered by his own admission.

The evening slogans echoed once again — some carrying conviction, some anger, some nothing at all. Vivaswa watched them together: a protest, a business, a performance, all occupying the same patch of ground. He looked toward Manish, then toward Suresh. Neither of them noticed him. He lowered his eyes.

For everyone else, he was just another ordinary protester. Exactly as he intended.

**Scene 3 – What Nobody Saw**

The protest ground had changed after sunset. The shouting had faded into scattered conversations; a few protesters slept on thin mats spread across the pavement, others huddled around disposable cups of tea discussing politics, cricket, and the uncertainty of tomorrow. Portable floodlights cast long shadows across the enclosure, turning the whole camp into something halfway between a fairground and a refugee shelter.

Vivaswa sat near one of the barricades, thumb moving across his phone screen. A volunteer passing with a stack of blankets glanced at the glow on his face and grinned. "Girlfriend?"

Vivaswa didn't look up. "Something like that."

The man laughed and moved on. Vivaswa kept typing, his eyes lifting every few seconds to sweep the camp — a volunteer distributing blankets, two media vans finally leaving for the night, a single police patrol vehicle completing another slow round before stopping nearly fifty metres away, a stray dog wandering between the sleeping bodies as though it, too, had somewhere important to be. He sent the message and locked the screen.

Two kilometres away, in a control room lit mostly by the blue glow of monitors, a constable's phone buzzed on the desk. He read the message twice, then carried it without comment to the officer standing near the whiteboard. Nobody in the room asked where it had come from. Nobody needed to. The number wasn't saved under any name.

Back at the barricade, Vivaswa's gaze settled on Manish, wrapped in a white sheet beneath the canopy, the cameras and the dramatic expressions long since gone for the day. A volunteer quietly helped him sit up. Manish reached into the side pocket of his backpack for the inhaler, took two puffs, exhaled slowly, and accepted a glass of warm water before the volunteer left again.

Vivaswa watched without expression. For nearly two weeks, nothing about the routine had changed — the inhaler always returned to the same side pocket, the backpack always placed beside the folded blanket. During late-night media interviews, someone else usually guarded it. After the interviews ended, nobody did.

A loud argument erupted near the entrance. Several protesters rushed toward it. "Come quickly! They're arguing with the police again!" a volunteer called out. Half the nearby group walked off toward the noise; the rest instinctively followed, drawn by the particular gravity of a crowd forming. Within seconds, the area around Manish's sleeping space was almost deserted.

Vivaswa remained seated. Five seconds. Ten. Fifteen. Nobody looked back.

He stood casually, stretched as though his legs had gone numb, and walked toward the water drums — past Manish's bedding, past the unattended backpack, past the folded blanket. He filled a paper cup and drank slowly.

Another protester smiled. "Couldn't sleep?"

"Too much noise," Vivaswa said, shrugging.

The man laughed. "That's what protests are."

"I suppose."

They spoke for barely half a minute. A few minutes later, Manish returned from speaking to another organiser and picked up the backpack without looking inside. As expected. Routine. Comfort. Habit.

Vivaswa threw the empty paper cup into a dustbin, his eyes drifting once toward the police barricades in the distance, then toward Suresh, speaking on the phone in hushed tones, then back to the crowd. Nothing about him looked different from the man who had walked to the water drums a few minutes earlier.

No one at the protest ground looked at him twice for the rest of the night.
